About

A living evidence hub for agroecological meta-analyses.

Built as evidence infrastructure rather than a single study, and global in scope, with no restriction on year, language or region.

The Hub answers one question, and keeps answering it as new evidence arrives: how effective are agroecological farming practices at improving agronomic, economic, environmental and social outcomes, compared with non-agroecological practices or with natural and semi-natural habitats?

It is developed by the Alliance of Bioversity International and CIAT within the CGIAR Multifunctional Landscapes Science Program, and is built as evidence infrastructure rather than a single study. Scope is global, with no restriction on year, language or region.

Why it exists

The evidence base has grown quickly, but it is fragmented. Many systematic syntheses have already extracted primary study data, and those datasets then sit unused in supplementary files. The Hub brings them together so that results can be compared across sources.

Evidence already extracted once should not have to be extracted again.

Who it is for

Researchers who need comparable results across sites and sources, and funders and policy teams who need to see where evidence is strong, thin or missing. Current downstream users include work on nutrition and environmental targeting in nine African countries, food system modelling, and European policy analysis.

The four outcome domains

  • Agronomic. Yield, biomass, land equivalent ratios, animal productivity.
  • Economic. Income, profit, gross margin, cost and benefit measures.
  • Environmental. Biodiversity, input use efficiency, greenhouse gases, soil carbon.
  • Social. Food security and nutrition, labour, equity and inclusion, resilience.

Social outcomes and under represented regions are named priorities, because that is where existing syntheses are thinnest.

What it is not

The Hub is not a recommendation engine. Effects depend on soil, rainfall, season and management, so a positive median does not mean a practice will work on a given farm. Agroecology is also a gradient rather than a binary, and source datasets rarely label their arms that way, so deciding which arm counts as agroecological is sometimes an analytical judgement rather than a fact inherited from the source.

Methodology

Evidence recycling, then one shared template.

The Hub does not read the primary literature afresh. It harvests the datasets earlier meta-analyses already extracted.

The Hub does not read the primary literature afresh. It harvests the primary study datasets that earlier meta-analyses already extracted, harmonises them into one shared template, and analyses them again, filling gaps by hand only where a source dataset was incomplete.

Step oneTwo registries

Syntheses are screened and routed. The second order registry records what each synthesis concluded. The first order registry holds the individual primary studies pulled out of those syntheses, which is where the measurements live. A synthesis can feed one registry or both.

Step twoHarmonisation

Each source dataset is mapped into the shared template. Studies already fully extracted by the source are harmonised by script. Partially covered studies are topped up by hand from the original paper. Practice names, outcomes, units, crops and animals come from a controlled vocabulary rather than free text.

Step threeOne row, one comparison

A row in the template is one control against treatment comparison for one outcome. Most fields come in control and treatment pairs, with study, site and outcome attributes shared. That is the unit a meta-analytic effect size needs.

Step fourEffect sizes

The analysis uses log response ratios and standardised mean differences, assigned per outcome by the vocabulary. For readability this site also shows the plain percentage change of the treatment mean against the control mean. Both travel with the reported means, dispersion and sample sizes, so you can recompute anything yourself.

Percentages are for reading. The log response ratio and its standard error are for analysis.

Standards

  • Reporting follows ROSES, the standard for systematic evidence syntheses in environmental management, not PRISMA.
  • Scoping uses the population, intervention, comparator, outcomes and context framework.
  • Metadata follows FAIR principles, aligned to the ROSES checklist with DataCite and Dublin Core.
  • The review is living: periodic re-search, re-screening and republication, each release tagged with a changelog and a persistent identifier.

Known limits

  • Coverage reflects which syntheses have been harmonised, not the whole literature.
  • Positive results are more likely to be published than null results.
  • Sites cluster around long running research stations.
  • Dispersion is not reported by every source, so standard errors are available for a subset of records.
  • Treatment does not automatically mean agroecological. Read the comparator field before pooling.

Get involved

Bring a dataset, extract a study, or reuse the table.

The Hub grows by contribution. Four ways to take part, in rising order of commitment.

The Hub grows by contribution, and the most valuable contribution is usually a dataset that already exists. Four ways to take part, in rising order of commitment.

Point us at a synthesis

If you published a meta-analysis with a primary study dataset, or you know of one, tell us. Harmonising an existing dataset adds far more evidence per hour than extracting papers one by one.

Extract a study

Where a source captured only part of what we need, records are completed by hand from the original paper into a standard workbook. Extractors work on one paper at a time and are credited.

Propose a vocabulary term

Practice and outcome names are controlled. When a term is missing, it is requested rather than invented, so that comparisons stay valid across sources.

Reuse the data

The current selection downloads as CSV from the Explore data page, with coordinates, means, dispersion, sample sizes and effect sizes. Tell us if you need a programmatic feed instead.
